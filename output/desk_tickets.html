Reflection

1. How did the agents perform on each ticket, and why?

I judged each ticket on three things: whether the facts were right, whether the money rules held, and how much work it took.

  • Ticket 102 (rent) was the strongest. Facilities found $2,400 due on 2026-09-02, and Accounting requested approval without paying. The final run took only 4 tool calls. Run 2 escalated because the rent tool couldn’t see the payment, but the Boss safely asked a human instead of requesting a second payment.
  • Ticket 101 (tee) was correct but inefficient. Every fact was right: 0 size S in stock and the vendor blocked by invoice 501. But it took 4 runs and 55 tool calls for a single $8 tee. Accounting repeated checks Inventory had already done, and Customer Service wrote 4 drafts.
  • Ticket 103 (hoodies) showed good judgment but was weak on cash. No agent invented a discount, and the margin math was correct. But no agent ever checked that the 12-hoodie restock ($264) was more than the $152 left in checking.

Overall: all three payments ($840, $2,400, and $8) were approved by a human before any cash moved. The weak spots were repeated work and no planning of cash across tickets.

2. How did the actual run compare to the expected plan?

  • Ticket 101: the agent path and both approvals matched my plan. It took 4 runs instead of 1 because the team stops at each approval. The dashboard paid the bills when I clicked Approve; the Accounting agent didn’t pay on its own.
  • Ticket 102: also matched my plan: Facilities, then Accounting, then my approval, and cash went down by $2,400. It took 3 runs because of the rent tool’s blind spot.
  • Ticket 103: the stock count (8 of 20), the $22 cost floor, and sending the discount to a human all matched. The only differences: the $264 restock was never requested or costed, and the discount ended as needs_human rather than an approval, because approvals only cover payments.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why?

Ticket 102. It’s a fixed chain of three tools: check rent, check cash, and request approval. Facilities called only one tool and handed off, and then Accounting called the same tool again. One agent would take about 4 calls instead of the 10 that were made. The safety comes from tool permissions and human approval, not from having separate agents, so one agent would have been just as safe.

4. Three new problems this team could solve with the tools I built

  1. An order for 30 Crest Mugs. Mugs are at 0 in stock, and their vendor, Elm City Gifts, has no unpaid invoices. Inventory finds the shortfall, and Accounting requests a $105 approval (30 × $3.50), which fits within the $152 left in checking. Customer Service drafts a reply with the expected 2026-09-03 arrival (3-day lead time).
  2. October rent with only $152 in checking. Facilities reports $2,400 due on 2026-10-02. The approval tool refuses because cash can’t cover it, and the Boss marks the ticket as blocked.
  3. The AI Club counteroffers $45 per hoodie. Accounting’s discount tool shows the price is still above the $22 cost. I record the decision on the dashboard, and Customer Service drafts the offer.

5. Three new problems this team could not solve, and what it would need

  1. Collecting payment from customers. No tool records a sale or adds cash, so there’s no revenue inflow. It would need a record-sale tool, a receive-stock tool, and possibly a Sales agent.
  2. Sending orders and messages. Everything stays a draft, and no tool can contact anyone. It would need send-purchase-order and send-message tools behind human review, plus a Communications agent.
  3. Planning cash ahead. The cash tool shows only today’s balance, and the Boss works one ticket at a time. It would need a cash-forecast tool and a Treasury agent that ranks bills across tickets.
